# Chapter 5 · The One Who Was Stuck

The fourth visitor came while Mei was wiping the counter. She had grown used to one thing: most people arrived just before closing, as if afraid to be seen by daylight believing in such things. This one was a young man in his early twenties, spine held stiff, like someone who had just escaped an interview — or was hurrying from one toward the next.

"I…" he opened his mouth, then closed it, as if checking whether the sentence was worth saying. "I have two offers. Both good. I made a pros-and-cons list, and the longer the list, the worse it got."

Mei set the cloth down. "Your name?"

"Theo," he said. "Just graduated."

She poured him warm water and slid it over. "Theo, you didn't come to buy a stone. You came stuck between two answers. Let's put the stones aside for a moment — what you're really afraid of isn't choosing wrong, is it?"

He blinked, as if something unspoken had been tapped gently. "My mom says I'm too picky. My dad says take the one that pays more. Me, I don't even know what I'm afraid of. I just know, the second I open that spreadsheet, my head goes static."

From the shelf Mei took a ring — a stone with a soft blue sheen set in a slim silver band, quiet as a small lake at night. The Rainbow Moonstone Intuition Ring. "This one doesn't choose for you," she said. "It only reminds you of one thing: the calm part of you that already knows the answer is older than that spreadsheet. You wear it not to decide at once. You wear it to slow down first, and hear that part."

Theo slid the ring onto his index finger; the blue light turned under the lamp. "And then?"

"Then, don't compare the offers." Mei took out another — the Aquamarine Flow Ring, a pale blue stone like a curve of still water held in the hand. "Water doesn't rush to decide where it flows. It just flows. First, write down what you actually fear — not 'A or B,' but 'what am I really afraid of.' Write it for me, and for yourself."

She handed him paper and pen. Theo chewed the cap, wrote, crossed out, wrote again. For a full ten minutes he stalled, then pushed the paper over.

One line: *Afraid of disappointing my parents.*

He stared at it, then laughed — a relieved, slightly wet laugh. "So it's not that I'm scared of choosing wrong. It's that I'm scared they'll think I'm a failure."

"That's the truth of it," Mei said. "You're afraid of their faces, not your own future. Those two things weigh differently. The stone can't choose for you. But when everything spins, it gives you something cool and honest to hold, so you don't float away."

Theo turned the moonstone ring half a turn, as if confirming it was still there. "If my mom knew I figured this out sitting in a crystal shop, she'd probably think I'm even more of a failure."

"Then don't tell her," Mei said. "Tell her which one you picked. The rest is between you and this ring."

When he left, both rings were on his hands, and his step was lighter than when he arrived. At the door he didn't pause. He walked straight into the evening wind.

Mei stood behind the counter for a while. She noticed that today, she hadn't once thought *when will this shop be cleared out.* She had only picked the cloth back up and kept wiping, wiping until her own shadow in the lamplight looked less in a hurry to leave.

The bell rang and she expected another customer; she looked up to find the owner of the bookstore next door. His curly hair carried a speck of ink; he held two coffees and grinned a little lazily. "Name's Noah," he said. "I'm across the street. Walked past your place — it's more mystical than my books."

Mei took the coffee. "Mystical is what gets people in. Books are too honest; people walk around them."

"I'm noting that for my store sign," Noah said, leaning in the doorway, his eyes sweeping the shelves. "Seriously though — are these things actually meaningful, or just pretty?"

"Both," Mei said. "Pretty is the threshold. Meaning is the person behind it. My grandmother's words, not mine."

"I like your grandmother," Noah said. "There's a market in the old town this weekend. I'll have a book stall. Want to come? Bring some of your 'meanings.' Let the street know there's still a lamp here that listens."

Mei looked down at the jade ring on her own hand — her grandmother's. Then she looked up, and for the first time, didn't hesitate.

"All right," she said. "A lamp's supposed to shine outward."

Outside, Maple Hollow's lights came on one by one. Above the doorframe, the obsidian bead waited in the shadows, as if listening too.
