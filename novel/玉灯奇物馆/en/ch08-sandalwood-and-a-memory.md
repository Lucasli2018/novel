# Chapter 8 · Sandalwood, and a Memory

## I. Sixty Years at the Same Table

After First Frost, the wind turned colder by the day. That afternoon the shop was quiet; Mei was putting the autumn incenses onto the shelf when the bell gave a soft ring and a silver-haired man stepped in.

He wore a washed-out navy sweater and held an equally worn soft felt hat, his knuckles clean, nails neatly trimmed — the kind of old man who still took good care of himself. He stood at the door for two seconds, as if confirming this was the right place, then walked slowly to the counter.

"Young lady," he began, his voice gentle with long disuse but not yet rusted. "I'm looking for an incense. One that… lets her be present."

Mei didn't rush to ask who *she* was. She looked at him first — his eyes were empty, but not the empty of grief. It was the small, warm place left after grief has been smoothed flat by years.

"Go on."

"My wife, Margot," he said her name with a slight move of the lips, like a word used for sixty years. "She's been gone a year and three months. When she was alive, there was always a thread of sandalwood in the house. She said, *this smell is like someone being here, not like a person keeping an empty house.*" He paused. "Now whenever I smell sandalwood, I feel she's still in the next room. Just hasn't walked over."

Mei turned and took a plain wooden box from the cabinet, lifted the lid — inside lay the **Sandalwood Five-Element Incense** (p02), five notes wrapped around a pure sandalwood base. Not sharp, not cloying. The kind of *presence* that sits across from you and has no intention of leaving.

"What sandalwood does," she slid the box toward him, "is stay. Warm, steady, lasting. It doesn't talk over you, doesn't hurry you. Light it, and the room feels like it gained a guest who isn't going anywhere."

The old man reached out, his fingertips brushing the box's edge, very light, like stroking the back of a hand.

"That's the one," he said.

## II. Light One, and Say Goodnight to Her

Mei led him to the incense corner and lit a stick, setting it on the low table between them. The smoke rose straight at first, then was kneaded by the draft from the window seam into a soft white line of moonlight.

The old man didn't speak right away. He just sat, eyes closed, letting the scent fill him. A long while passed before he began — and what he said was no eulogy, only things far too small to matter to anyone but them.

"She'd always set a cup of tea by my elbow when I was writing my papers. It would go cold, and she'd forget to change it, and I'd forget to drink it. By the time I noticed, it was just a tepid cup." He smiled a little. "When we fought, neither of us spoke first. But every night at bedtime, two hands would reach out at the same moment to turn off the lamp. Neither would admit fault, yet neither could bear to leave the other in the dark."

The smoke climbed; his tears fell silently, soaking into the sweater's knit, soon a small dark circle. But his shoulders were loose — not in pain, but in finally having somewhere to set it down.

"I used to think remembering meant going to the grave," he said softly. "Today I understand — the scent keeps her company for me. She's gone, yet the smell is here, like she's still in the next room, just hasn't come over."

Mei said nothing. She only nudged the incense closer to him, letting that white drift nearer.

When he'd settled, she took down a **Vermillion Protection Bracelet** (p12) from the shelf — red agate and cinnabar beads, a small stubborn fire in the lamplight. In East Asian custom, this red is the color of protection.

"Take this with you," she said. "Not to forget her. To remind you — the warmth you gave her, she gave back to you. Wear it like a shield, and carry her warmth as you go on."

The old man took it, closed it in his palm, his thumb slowly working the beads, as if confirming a weight.

"Sixty years at the same table," he murmured. "Turns out it never ended."

## III. What She Came to Understand

After he left, the shop fell quiet. Mei cleared the ash from the low table and found herself wanting the notebook. She brightened the lamp, took *The Curio Record* from the drawer, and turned to the page where a sprig of dried calming herb was pressed — her grandmother's line, read many times, yet seeming new today:

**Carry feeling through scent; connect hearts through things.**

She had always taken it for a pretty phrase, a dignified way for an old woman to justify her trade. But now, watching the still-warm stick on the table, remembering the old man's loosened shoulders as he held the bracelet, she understood at last — her grandmother hadn't meant the scent reached the dead, or that objects held power. She meant: **some things a person never says out loud, and the smell catches them; some ties they can't let go of, and the object keeps them.** 

A curio doesn't love for you, doesn't remember for you. It only hands back, steady and whole, the feeling you already carried.

She moved the box of sandalwood incense up from beneath the counter to the shelf that "most deserved to be seen" — beside the jade lantern. She had once hidden it below, afraid of drowning in her grandmother's lingering warmth and never deciding. Now she wasn't afraid. This scent was her grandmother's whole teaching on *how to keep someone company*.

That night, before she blew out the lamp, a faint, long thread of sandalwood rose beside the lantern — as if her grandmother still sat in the room, turning the pages of an old book.

The obsidian bead above the doorframe hung quiet in the light, like it was listening too.

She thought, for a moment, of that ring of pink scaffolding across the street: the man over there could never give this kind of *presence*. He could sell all the *blessed* and *healing* wares he liked, yet he could not sell a single stick of incense that made someone feel *she's still in the next room* — because that thing was never on any shelf.

The lamp did not go out. The scent did not scatter.

And she, now, knew for whom this lantern would keep burning.
