# Chapter 11 · The Scent That Led the Way

## I. The Notebook by Lamplight

The morning light had not yet climbed over the roofline at the corner of the street, but the Jade Lantern Curio was already glowing.

Not from electricity. From the small jade lamp.

When Mei pushed the door open, there it was again, warm and unhurried, as if someone had arrived before her—or as if it had never quite gone out.

She set her bag on the counter and did not switch on the lights. She stood before the jade lamp for a while. Its shadow fell thin and golden across the back of her hand. Three months ago, the first time she had pushed this door open, it had been the same—the lamp still lit, her grandmother newly gone, the air carrying the last breath of old wood and sandalwood.

Today was different. Today she had not come to pack.

Mei pulled the leather-bound notebook, *The Curio Record*, toward her and turned to the last page. Her grandmother Lin Suyin's handwriting, every stroke pressed into the paper as if carved:

> If the lamp remains but the cup is lost, follow the light to find it.
> It was taken, not misplaced. He who took it knows the light.

"Follow the light," she read aloud. The words came back to her, soft, from the empty shop.

Her fingers moved back a few pages. On an earlier leaf, her grandmother had written:

> Everywhere the light has walked, I left a mark. The tea at the flea market. The smoke at the incense shop north of the city. And...

The ink broke off there. As if the sentence had been left unfinished on purpose, or as if she had chosen to leave it blank.

Mei stared at that *and* for a long time.

She thought of the empty velvet box in the cellar—the small jade cup that had once stood beside the lamp was gone. The notebook said *taken*, not *stolen*. He who took it knew the light.

"The tea at the flea market," she copied into her own curio journal. "Start there."

The obsidian bead still hung above the doorframe, untouched since that night in chapter three. In the morning light it shone black and bright, like a piece of night someone had pressed pause on.

## II. The Scent of Focus

Not long after opening, a young writer came in.

He carried a notebook, his fingers stained with ink. He had been stuck at the beginning of a manuscript for two weeks, he said. "Like there's a door. I know something is behind it. I just can't push it open."

Mei did not reach for a stone right away. "Before you write," she asked, "is there any fixed thing you do?"

"No." He shook his head. "I sit down. Then I stare."

"Then let's make you one."

From the cabinet she took a box of incense sticks and chose a pale-gold one—pine and citrus, clear and bright, like opening a window. It was AuraSage's **Pine & Citrus Ritual Incense** (p07). The name sounded fancy; the meaning was plain: give a task a switch that says *begin*.

"Not to wake you up. A ritual," Mei said, setting the stick in a small burner. "Every time you sit to write, light one first. Let your nose start the shift, and your hands follow."

The writer lit it, doubtful. Pale smoke rose. The clean bitterness of pine wrapped the sweetness of citrus, spreading slowly through the fragrance room. He closed his eyes and breathed. Then he said, suddenly, "What I'm stuck on isn't that I can't write. It's that I'm afraid to begin—afraid that the first line will be wrong."

"Then don't write it well," Mei said, and slid the **Clear Quartz Focus Bracelet** (p49) across the counter—one her grandmother had left behind, the very piece that had steadied the insomnic teacher Claire in chapter one. "Wear it. Write the first rotten sentence. The crystal doesn't judge you. It only shows you to yourself."

He put it on and picked up the pen. Three minutes later, the first line walked onto the page: *I grew up in a house that always leaked when it rained.*

Mei stepped back to the lamp and did not disturb him. She knew this moment—the object did not help the person. The person used the object to set their own panic down, somewhere they could begin.

## III. The Address of the Market

Near noon, Noah arrived with two coffees, his curls still dusted with bookshop grey.

"Heard you're going treasure-hunting?" He grinned. "I checked. There's a flea market west of the city on Saturdays."

Mei turned the open notebook toward him. "My grandmother said 'follow the light to find it.' The clues are scents—the tea at the flea market, the smoke at the incense shop north of the city."

Noah leaned in to read the line that broke off at *and*, and whistled. "Your grandmother arranges even her disappearances like puzzles."

"Not a disappearance," Mei said, pointing at the last page. "A taking. He who took it knows the light."

"Then we'll go find the one who knows the light." Noah set a coffee by her hand. "I'll come Saturday. Two people look better than one person brooding at a lamp."

Mei looked at him, and for a moment the shop felt like more than hers. The lamp, the notebook, the obsidian bead above the door—and now this man with ink on his cuffs—had all become part of *following the light*.

She polished the jade lamp and wrote in her curio journal:

> Day one. Following the pine-citrus scent, to the flea market.

Outside, the autumn sun of Maple Hollow finally cleared the roofline and fell across the jade lamp. In its glow, the line *follow the light* seemed to brighten, just a little, on its own.

Above the door, the obsidian bead hung, quiet.
