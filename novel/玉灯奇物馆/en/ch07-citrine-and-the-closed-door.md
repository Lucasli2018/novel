# Chapter 7 · Citrine and the Closed Door

## I. Anxiety Behind the Rolling Shutter

After that ring of pink neon scaffolding went up across the street, Mei slept better than she had in weeks. She'd stopped wondering *how long the shop could last* — once someone put the fake version on display, she could finally see the real thing she was meant to guard.

That afternoon she walked over for coffee. Rounding the corner, she found a little shop with its rolling shutter pulled down to half-mast, and a face leaning out from the gap.

"Miss Lin! Lucky I caught you," said Priya, who ran the corner café, a knot of *can't-make-the-numbers-work* permanently creased between her brows. "People say you know… those fortune stones? My business is so dead I'm about to eat the rent."

Mei ducked under the shutter and followed her in.

A stale, trapped smell met them — not dirty, just *unmoving*. The shutter stayed half-raised, so the light never finished coming in. Behind the counter, cartons stacked chest-high; the aisle narrowed to a single shoulder's width; even the hanging lamp was half-blocked by a box. Standing inside felt like being gently pressed by four walls. Mei reached out and touched the shutter's cold aluminum edge; a film of dust came off on her fingertip.

Priya tugged a red string from her neck; on it hung a plastic "prosperity stone," its color too bright to be true. "I tried this. Nothing. Give me a real one — citrine. I looked it up. For wealth."

Mei didn't reach for a stone. She walked the shop first, her fingertips brushing the dusty counter, and stopped before a **Cloud Metal Feng Shui Pendant** (p15) — a hollow silver cloud, the classic Taoist sign that *good fortune is arriving*.

"What you want isn't citrine," she said, lifting the pendant into the light. "It's the thought that *good luck is on its way*. But that thought needs a door it can actually walk through."

## II. Open the Door, Let the Air In

Priya blinked. "The door's open."

"Half," Mei said, pointing at the shutter overhead. "Half the light gets in, and so do half the customers. You set a stone on the altar but you blocked the path to it."

She didn't lecture. She moved. Hung the cloud pendant above the register — *let it remind you, from today, that you're open*. Then she had Priya help haul the blocking cartons aside, aisle by inch opening up; finally the two of them heaved the shutter all the way up, and the afternoon poured in like water, landing on the freshly wiped counter.

Priya let out a long breath, lungs filling for what felt like the first time.

Mei brought out a **Selenite Purity Wand Pendant** (p50), a small column of light caged in silver, milky white in the sun — a frozen sliver of moonlight. "Clear your own space first," she set it on the emptied windowsill, "and then the air can come. Selenite holds no dirt — look at it once a day, and let it remind you not to let the clutter pile back up, in here or in your head."

Priya's eyes lit. She turned, grabbed a rag, and scrubbed the grimy glass door until it shone, laughing as she wiped. "How long since I cleaned this door?"

Mei watched her, and thought of the line in her grandmother's notebook: *objects connect people to memory.* Today this object connected to nothing from the past. It connected to tomorrow.

"When you're ready," she said on her way out, "hang a string of five-emperor coins above the door. That's when order takes a shape. But that's a later story."

— The five-emperor coins were another custom entirely, a thing to wait for until the shop had found its feet. She tucked the thought away, like a seed not yet planted.

## III. I Stay

In the days that followed, Priya really did come back. She said customers had suddenly returned; someone had complimented how bright the place felt, how easy it was to step inside. She'd brought a bag of her own roasted beans and pressed it into Mei's hands: "So your lantern can smell a little coffee too." She didn't know it — what had changed wasn't her luck. It was the door someone had finally pulled open.

Mei saw her out, then turned back to find the jade lantern still burning.

That night she opened the drawer. On top lay her *closing checklist* — what to pack, which day to hang the For Sale sign. For weeks she'd been crossing items off, one by one, until only the last line remained: *this shop, eventually, must be cleared.*

She took up the pen and crossed it out. The nib paused on the stroke, heavy.

From *stay one week* to today, she'd lost count of how many sevens had passed. The lamp never went out. People came, and left lighter than they arrived. She flipped the notebook to a fresh page and wrote:

**Temporary stay → running the shop.**

Outside, the pop-up's pink glow burned especially bright tonight, almost a challenge. Before she blew out the lantern, Mei glanced at it one last time — still lit, the way it had been in her grandmother's day. And she understood, at last, that line: *the lamp does not go out; the thread is not yet cut.* Not that the lamp couldn't live without the person — but that the person couldn't bear to let it go.

And she, now, couldn't bear to.
