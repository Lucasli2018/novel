# Chapter 2 · The Rose That Wasn't for Love

The second visitor came in the early dusk, while Mei was still arguing with the window latch and a stack of unsorted receipts. He was a man in his thirties with the careful posture of someone who spent his days at a desk, and he approached the counter the way other people approached a library desk—quietly, as if afraid the question itself might be embarrassing.

"Do you have rose quartz?" he asked. "For, um. For love."

Mei set the receipts down. "For love," she repeated, gentle, not mocking.

He nodded, eyes on the glass case. "I read it helps. With the heart. Opening it. I thought—" He stopped, laughed a little, colorless. "I don't know what I thought."

"You came to the right kind of shop to not know what you thought." Mei came around the counter. "Let me show you what I have. Though I'll warn you, the quartz doesn't open anything by itself. My grandmother used to say the stone just sits there looking honest. The opening is yours."

She unlocked the case and drew out a tray. There was rose quartz in it, pale as a held breath, but she set it aside and instead lifted two other strands. "Before we talk about love," she said, "tell me who you're hoping will feel closer."

The man—Daniel, he said his name was—blinked at her. "That's a weird question for a jewelry shop."

"It's not a jewelry shop. It's a curio. Different thing." She nodded at the stool by the counter. "Sit. Tell me about the person."

And slowly, the way Claire had the night before, Daniel unfolded. His mother had died six weeks ago. Lung, fast. He'd been "fine" at work, fine in meetings, fine filling the dishwasher—and then not fine, at 2 a.m., standing in the kitchen that still smelled faintly of her lavender hand cream. He'd googled "crystals for grief" because he didn't know how else to look for her. Rose quartz had been her stone. She'd kept a raw chunk on the windowsill and called it her "heart opener."

"I don't even want to date anyone," he said, suddenly embarrassed. "That's the stupid part. I just—I keep reaching for my phone to text her. And she's not there. The quartz was hers. I thought if I had one too, maybe—" He gestured, helpless. "I don't know. Less alone in the chest."

Mei was quiet for a moment. Then she pushed the rose quartz further aside and laid the first strand in his palm: a lotus calm bracelet, small silver lotus beads strung on a dark cord, cool and light.

"This one's not for romance," she said. "Lotus grows in mud. It doesn't pretend the mud isn't there. It just blooms anyway. My grandmother kept one of these by the door for everyone who came in carrying something heavy they hadn't named yet. She'd say: bloom where you're planted, even on the hard days. Your mother's rose quartz opened her heart to living people. This one's for you, for the part of you that's still in the mud and allowed to be."

Daniel turned the bracelet over in his fingers. "That's not what I came in for."

"I know." Mei smiled, the small one she was learning. "That's usually the point."

She laid the second strand beside it—a serene water pearl bracelet, soft round beads the color of a still pond. "Water, in the old framing, is for what flows and what's held. Grief is both. You don't empty it. You learn the shape of the vessel. Wear this when the 2 a.m. kitchen gets loud. Not to forget her. To remember her without drowning."

He looked at the two bracelets, then at her. "You're not going to tell me they'll fix it."

"I'm going to tell you they won't," Mei said. "Nothing in this shop fixes anything. The lamp didn't fix Claire's sleep. It just gave her a room to be awake in. These give you a small, honest thing to hold when the chest gets tight. That's the whole trick."

Daniel closed his hand around the lotus bracelet. "Can I—can I sit a minute? With the lamp?"

"Door's open. Lamp's lit." Mei stepped back to the receipts, and let him have the quiet.

He stayed maybe twenty minutes. When he left, the bracelet was on his wrist, the pearl one in his coat pocket, and he shook her hand like she'd done him a larger favor than she had. At the door he paused.

"My mom would've liked this place," he said. "She liked things that meant more than they cost."

"Then she'd have liked my grandmother," Mei said. "They'd have traded stones for an afternoon."

After he was gone, Mei wrote his visit into the Curio Codex—not the notebook, a new one she'd started, because the old one still frightened her a little. *Daniel. Lotus for the mud he's blooming through. Pearl for the grief he's learning to hold. Came for love, meant he came for his mother.* She read it over, then added, under it: *Not every rose is for love. Some are just for still being here.*

Outside, the streetlamp came on. The jade lamp answered from the counter, steady and warm, as if it had been waiting all along.
