# Chapter 4 · The Notebook in the Basement

The obsidian bead still hung above the doorframe that morning, exactly where she had tied it the night before. Lin Mei looked up at it when she opened the shop, the way you check on a guest who shouldn't be there but has clearly moved in. She didn't touch it. She only rolled the shutter all the way up and let Tuesday's light pour in.

Then she turned, and for the first time, walked toward the basement stairs.

She hadn't opened that door since she arrived. While Grandmother Suyin was alive, the basement had been off-limits — "what's down there will keep, it doesn't need you" — and Mei, who had always found it musty, had been a little afraid of it. Afraid of what, she couldn't say. Afraid, maybe, of meeting a grandmother she wasn't ready to hold.

The stairs were wood and complained under every step, as if the house were counting her. The air cooled as she went down, carrying a smell she knew: old timber, damp, and the faintest ember of agarwood — her grandmother's agarwood. She had caught it in Suyin's room before; not the smoke of burning incense, but the scent the wood itself had memorized.

The basement was small. A little jade-like lamp, unplugged, sat on a chest by the window, and the daylight barely reached the corners. Against the wall stood a camphorwood chest, its lid tagged in Suyin's fading ink: Spring · Crystal. Summer · Incense. Autumn · Jade. Winter · Bronze. Beside it, a row of curio boxes, each tied with cloth and named in her hand — a whole quiet neighborhood of objects, every one of them properly settled.

Mei ran a finger along the labels — not only the seasons, but small notes in Suyin's hand: *for the one who can't sleep. for the one who left. for the one who finally said it.* The shop, it turned out, had been a filing cabinet for other people's hearts.

She crouched before the chest and, for a moment, only looked. Things remembered this carefully by one person were, by themselves, a kind of comfort.

She opened the top box. Not crystal — a small parcel of agarwood chips (Agarwood Deep Root Incense). She lifted one piece; the grain was dense, and close up it gave off a sweetness that time had pressed flat. She had burned calming herb incense in the scenting nook, but she had never really smelled her grandmother's agarwood. Now she did — it was like stepping inside a tree that had been wounded for years and had finally gone still. She didn't light it. She only set the chip beneath the lamp and let the smell spread, slow and low.

In a hidden compartment of the chest lay a notebook titled *Records of Curios*. The pages had come loose, some tucked elsewhere. Mei spread them across her lap and read.

The first line she already knew from the lamp: *The lamp does not go out; the thread is not yet cut.* Below it, Suyin's hand — slow, steady, as if afraid of disturbing something:

*Curios connect people with memory. They are not merchandise. A stone treated only as goods is dead. Held in a hand, and thought of someone, it lives.*

Mei's throat tightened. Everything she had done for three days — listening first, before handing over a stone — turned out not to be her own invention. Her grandmother had written it here, long before.

A few pages on, another line:

*I do not sell cures. I only lend a stone until a person remembers they were whole. Then they return it, or keep it — either way, the work is done.*

Mei bit her lip. This was exactly the line she had been most afraid of crossing all week: no exaggeration, no promises, only staying with someone until they said the thing that needed saying. Her grandmother had written it down as a rule.

Midway through, a line stopped her fingers.

*A capital brand came courting. They wanted to swallow the shop and sell curios as fast-consumer "spiritual" goods. I refused. What they wanted was the price of the words "Eastern mystery." Not the people beneath them. A shop may be small. It must not be hollow.*

Beneath it, Suyin had drawn a small lamp, and beside the lamp, four words: *The lamp stands; the person stands.*

Mei pressed that page to her chest and did not move for a long moment. She understood the obsidian bead by the door all at once — her grandmother hadn't left a stone. She had left a tone of voice that said *I'm still here.* And the brand that had wanted to buy the whole shop and turn its objects into labels — Suyin had said no, decades ago.

She kept turning pages, and at the bottom of the chest found a velvet box holding a jade ring (Jade Growth Ring). The face was small, and very quietly green — the green of early spring just before it wakes. Mei knew this ring. Her grandmother had worn it always; as a child Mei had stared at that green dot more times than she could count. She slipped it on. The knuckle still carried the thin callus of years spent handling objects, and yet the ring fit exactly, as if it had been waiting for this hand.

She didn't cry. She only turned the ring half a turn, the way you turn an unspoken *I'll take it from here* inward, where it could stay.

She closed the chest, went upstairs, and made one phone call.

Old Zhou was a craftsman in Chinatown; most of Suyin's stock had come through him. Through the phone came the sound of planing wood, a soft shhh, as if another shop were breathing quietly in the background.

"Mei-yat?" Zhou knew her voice. "Your grandmother told me, before she left, that you'd come round to it in the end."

"Uncle Zhou, I want to restock," Mei said. "And I'd like you to go through what's in the shop with me — tell me what's real, and what…"

"What your grandmother picked, it's real," Zhou cut in, flat as someone remarking on the weather. "She chose with her eyes and her memory both. You just put them out. If anything won't sit right, ask me."

"And," Mei hesitated, "did she ever… turn down a big company that wanted to buy the shop?"

The line was quiet for two beats. The planing didn't stop.

"There was that," Zhou said. "That company changed its name later. Still in the 'Eastern' business, only flashier now. Your grandmother said it wasn't inheritance. It was a veneer. She'd rather the shop stayed small."

Mei closed her hand around the ring. So her grandmother hadn't stood alone. On this street, someone else could catch the words she hadn't finished saying.

"Uncle Zhou, I'll come by in a couple of days."

"Bring tea."

After she hung up, Mei went back to the counter and carefully photographed the *Records* page about the refused acquisition, saving it to her phone. She wasn't sure why she kept it. Only a feeling: this wasn't over.

Outside, old Maple Hollow dimmed toward evening. She looked once more at the obsidian bead by the door, then turned and lit the jade lamp.

Beneath it, for the first time, she didn't feel the shop was waiting to be cleared out. It was waiting for her — to stay.
