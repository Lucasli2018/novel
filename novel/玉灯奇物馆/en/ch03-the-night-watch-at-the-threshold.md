# Chapter 3 · The Night-Watch at the Threshold

The third visitor was not a stranger to the street, only to the shop. Mrs. Okafor owned the bakery two doors down, and Mei had watched her through the window for three mornings now—apron, flour on the wrists, the particular economy of someone who had done the same forty loaves before sunrise for twenty years.

She came in at noon, which meant the lamp was dark and the shop looked, Mei thought, exactly like what it was: a closed curio with a young woman pretending to be organized.

"I heard you help people," Mrs. Okafor said without preamble. She was a round woman with capable hands and a worried crease between her brows. "The schoolteacher, Claire. She slept last night. First time in weeks. She told the coffee line this morning that it was your shop."

Mei set down the inventory she wasn't really doing. "Claire came by. We sat. That's most of what happened."

"She says the lamp stayed lit the whole time she was here." Mrs. Okafor's tone was careful, the tone of a practical person testing whether the other person is a fraud. "I'm not here for the lamp."

"Good. It's not for sale."

"I'm here because of the door." Mrs. Okafor glanced back at the entrance, as if checking it was still there. "Two shops over, the florist got broken into. Not much taken. But since then I feel—" she chose the word like it embarrassed her "—watched. At the threshold. When I lock up at night, I stand there a second too long. Like something's deciding whether to follow me in."

Mei nodded slowly. She did not say *there's no such thing*. Her grandmother had taught her that what people feel at their own threshold is real to them, and that was the only part that mattered.

"My grandmother kept a stone by every door," Mei said. "Not to stop anyone. To remind the room who it belongs to." She went to the shelf and took down a strand of black obsidian beads, smooth and dense and darker than the wood behind it. "This is obsidian. In the old East-Asian framing, it's a stone of protection and grounding—a quiet guardian for uncertain days. You don't wear it to frighten anything off. You wear it to remember your own boundaries are intact."

Mrs. Okafor turned it in her palm. The beads were cool, heavier than they looked. "And the threshold? Do I hang it on the door?"

"You can," Mei said. "Or wear it, which my grandmother preferred—because the boundary that matters most is the one you carry. But for a door specifically, she'd tie a single bead above the frame. One bead. Enough to say *this is mine*."

The baker's brows eased a fraction. "One bead. I can do one bead."

Mei laid a second strand beside it—earth root meditation beads, a warm brown wood with the grain of river stone, strung to be held rather than worn. "And these are for you, not the door. Earth root. A string of stillness to hold when the world spins. When you're standing at your own threshold a second too long, take these in your hand. Not to stop the feeling. To stay in it without drowning."

Mrs. Okafor looked at the two strands a long time. Then, surprising Mei, she laughed—a short, surprised sound, almost relieved. "My grandmother was Yoruba. She kept a stone by her door too. Different stone, same idea. She'd say the house knows who's welcome."

"Then your grandmother and mine would've understood each other," Mei said. "Stones are just honest objects. The knowing is yours."

"I'll take both," Mrs. Okafor said. "The bead for the door, the wood for me. And tell Claire the lamp's welcome anytime. This street's been nervous since the break-in. We look out for each other, or we don't make it through the winter."

She paid, awkwardly, with a ten and a thanks, and left with the obsidian in her coat and the wood beads in her palm. At the door she did pause—but only to smile, and then she was gone into the noon glare.

Mei stood at the threshold herself for a moment, the way you do after someone names a thing true. She looked down the street, at the florist's darkened window two doors over, at the ordinary honest light of a Tuesday.

Then she went back inside and, for the first time, lit the jade lamp in daylight—not because anyone was coming, but because the room wanted it, and because she was beginning to understand that a threshold guarded is also a threshold kept open.

That night, when she went to lock up, she found a single black bead already waiting on the shelf by the door. She hadn't put it there. She didn't remember her grandmother putting it there, either.

She tied it above the frame anyway, and left the lamp on.
