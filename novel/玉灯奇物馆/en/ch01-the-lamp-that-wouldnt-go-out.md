# Chapter 1 · The Lamp That Wouldn't Go Out

The key fit the lock the way it always had, with a small reluctant catch before it turned. Mei stood on the threshold of the Jade Lantern Curio and told herself, for the fourth time that night, that she was only here to sort through things. Box them. Tag them. Decide what to keep and what to let go. Three months since Grandmother Suyin had passed, and the shop had been breathing dust in the dark ever since.

She had not expected the light.

The door opened with its old creak, the sound she used to hear from the upstairs apartment when Nai Nai was still up reading. Mei stepped in. The front room smelled of cold wood and the last ghost of sandalwood, and through the single window facing Maple Hollow's quiet main street, a streetlamp laid a pale stripe across the floor. And there, on the counter at the back of the room, the jade lamp was glowing.

It was not supposed to glow. The lamp was a carving, a small ornate lantern cut from a single pale-green stone, no taller than her forearm. Nai Nai had kept it on the counter as a thing to look at, not a thing that worked. Yet a soft amber warmth pooled inside the carved panels, and when Mei walked closer and laid two fingers against the side, the stone was faintly warm, as if someone had only just set it down.

She had blown it out. She was certain of that. The last evening she had spent here, the evening after the funeral when the relatives had gone and the silence had gotten too loud, she had stood exactly here and pinched the little flame between thumb and finger. She remembered the warmth on her skin. She remembered leaving it dark.

But the lamp did not care what she remembered.

Mei let her hand fall. Around her the shop settled into its ordinary shapes: the glass case with its rows of tumbled stones, the low shelves of incense and sealed jars, the folded screens painted with cranes. Dust drifted in the beam from the window, slow and unconcerned. The place was exactly as Nai Nai had left it, which was to say exactly as it had always been, which was to say not hers.

She had lived in three cities since she was eighteen. She had a degree in design and a job she had quit by email. Maple Hollow was the town she visited for holidays and funerals, and she had told herself, on the train up, that a week was enough. A week to close the account, to call the estate people, to tell the regulars the curio was closing. Then back to wherever came next.

The lamp flickered, steady as a held breath.

"All right," Mei said aloud, to the empty room and the warm stone. "A week. I'll give you a week of tidying, and then we talk about letting go."

She set her bag by the counter and began, not with boxes but with the lamp. She wiped its base with the hem of her sleeve, and the carved cranes seemed to lift a little in the light. On the shelf behind it sat Nai Nai's notebook, the one everyone in the neighborhood called *the Curio Codex*, though it had no title on its cover. Mei had not opened it yet. Some evenings the sight of it was simply too much—a whole life written in a hand she would never hear again.

She left it closed.

The work was soothing, in the way only meaningless work can be. She sorted a tray of rose quartz by size. She wiped the glass of the display case until her own face looked back at her, dark-haired, twenty-six, with the thin calluses on her index finger that came from years of pencils and pens and the edge of a drafting knife. She found a stick of sandalwood five-element incense she did not remember stocking and set it aside for later. By the time the streetlamp outside went orange and then went dark, the front room looked almost tended.

She was folding a length of bubble wrap when the bell above the door gave its small, surprised ring.

Mei straightened. It was well past ten. Through the glass she saw a figure on the step, shoulders drawn up against the cold, one hand raised as if she had been about to knock and thought better of it. The woman was young, maybe Mei's age, in a coat buttoned wrong, with the kind of shadows under her eyes that no amount of sleep had been able to erase.

Mei almost said *we're closed*. The words were right there. Instead she found herself unlocking the door.

The woman stepped in like someone entering a library after dark—careful, half-expecting to be shooed out. Up close Mei saw the tremble in her hands, the way her fingers knotted and unknotted the strap of a canvas tote.

"I'm sorry," the woman said. Her voice was hoarse, as if she had been talking or crying or both. "I know it's late. I just—your grandmother told me, if I ever couldn't sleep, to come look at the lamp."

Mei closed the door behind her against the wind. "You knew my grandmother?"

"Claire. I taught across the street, at the elementary school. Mrs. Lin—she used to let me sit in here after hard days. Said the light was better than any pill." Claire's laugh came out thin and cracked. "That was her joke. She knew I didn't take pills. She just meant… sit. Breathe. Look at the jade."

Mei looked at the lamp, still glowing on the counter, and felt something loosen in her chest she had not known was tied. Nai Nai had done this—sat with strangers, let them borrow her quiet. It was the most like her mother's mother thing Mei had encountered since the funeral.

"I'm Mei," she said. "I'm—I'm sorting the shop. I wasn't planning to open it again."

"I'm not here to buy anything." Claire's eyes shone suddenly, bright with the kind of exhaustion that tips easily into tears. "I haven't slept in eleven days. Not really. I lie down and the ceiling turns into every mistake I ever made, and my grandmother—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Your grandmother said, when that happens, come to the lamp. So I came. I don't even know if you're open. I just needed to be in the room with it."

Eleven days. Mei had read the brochures; she knew insomnia was a doctor's business, not a curio shop's. She knew the responsible thing was to say *go home, call someone, rest*. But Claire was already lowering herself onto the stool by the counter, looking at the jade lamp the way a drowning person looks at shore, and Mei heard herself say:

"Stay. Just for a little."

She led Claire past the display case, into the back room Nai Nai had called the Incense Nook—a smaller space with a low table, floor cushions, and a single round window that let in the night. Mei lit a small burner and, almost without deciding to, took from the tray one of her grandmother's clear-quartz focus bracelets—a strand of faceted beads that caught the lamp's glow and went milky and soft, like a held breath made visible.

"My grandmother taught me this isn't magic," Mei said, because someone had to say it, because she believed it. "The stone doesn't *do* anything. But she said clear quartz is for seeing yourself clearly. For holding up a plain mirror. Sometimes what keeps you awake is just not having looked at the thing straight on."

Claire nodded, though Mei wasn't sure she was listening. Mei struck a match to a stick of sandalwood five-element incense—five scents at once, warm wood and something like a small balanced world. The smoke rose in a thin thread and slowly unfurled across the ceiling.

"Sit," Mei said. "Close your eyes. Don't try to sleep. Just tell the lamp what's true."

For a long moment Claire only breathed. The incense wound around them. Then, haltingly, she began to talk—about the student she had failed to reach, about the apartment that felt too big since her own grandmother had died in the spring, about the particular terror of the 3 a.m. hour when the day's shape was gone and only the worrying remained. Mei said little. She had learned, in design reviews and in life, that most people did not need answers. They needed someone to stay in the room while they found the answer themselves.

The quartz rested between them, white and patient. The lamp's light threw Claire's shadow large upon the wall, and the shadow, at least, looked at peace.

When Claire finally opened her eyes, the hard line of her mouth had softened. "I should go," she said, but she made no move. Then: "That's the first time in days I felt my shoulders drop."

"Then the lamp did its job," Mei said, and was surprised to mean it.

At the door Claire turned back, and in the amber spill from the shop she gave Mei the smallest real smile of the evening. "Thank you. For not sending me away."

"Thank my grandmother," Mei said. "She left the light on."

After the door closed, Mei stood in the quiet and let the incense finish its slow burning. She moved to blow out the lamp—and stopped. The notebook lay open on the counter where the draft of evening air must have flipped it, though she could have sworn it had been closed. The first line, in Nai Nai's precise hand, caught the lamplight:

*灯不灭，缘不绝* — *The lamp does not go out; the thread is not yet cut.*

Mei read it twice. She did not know what thread her grandmother had meant. She only knew that the lamp was warm, and Claire's shoulders had dropped, and for the first time in three months the shop felt less like a burden left to her and more like a door someone had left open on purpose.

She pinched the flame out, the way she had thought she did before. Darkness came in soft and complete.

Then, through the glass of the front window, she saw it: another shadow, another standing figure on the step, waiting in the night for the lamp to come back on.

Mei set the notebook down and did not blow the match out entirely. She lit the jade lamp again, and its small warm glow climbed the walls of the Jade Lantern Curio, and somewhere in Maple Hollow a second person who could not sleep began, very quietly, to hope.
