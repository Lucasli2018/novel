# Chapter 9 · The Copycat Down the Street

## I. The Pink-Purple Light

Elena all but ran in. The community weaver was usually quiet, but that day her face was flushed red; she dropped a paper bag on the counter. "Mei, look! The new shop across the street is so pretty… I bought this, they said it's blessed, but I wore it all night and felt nothing."

Inside the bag was a plasticky crystal pendant on a cheap elastic cord, its tag printed with a few misspelled Chinese characters and a jumble of Sanskrit. Mei picked it up; one touch, and it was wrong — not the weight a stone should have.

"How much?"
"Eighty-nine," Elena said. "Way cheaper than yours."
Mei didn't take the bait. She walked to the window and lifted a corner of the curtain. The empty storefront across the street had changed: pink-purple neon spelled out *AYTHER · Eastern Mystery* (misspelled, with an extra H), the window stuffed with garish "crystals," a sign bearing a freakish symbol — an eye inside a bagua — mashing Tibetan, Taoist, whatever, into one pot, smeared under the word *healing*.

"They call it an 'energy matrix,'" Elena whispered. "Say it fixes your luck, cures insomnia."

Mei set the pendant back in the bag and slid it over. "Elena, this won't help you sleep. It's not even real crystal. It just… printed the word *East* onto a product."

## II. Wrong Is Wrong

She called Zhou. Half an hour later he pushed through the door, trailing the clean scent of planed wood. One glance at the pendant and his brows knotted.

"This is desecration," Zhou turned it over and over, knuckles tapping the counter. "The bagua is about orientation, not a charm. Sanskrit is scripture, not decoration. They've torn all of it from its roots and pasted it onto money's face." He looked up. "Xiao Mei, your grandmother hated this most."

Mei nodded. She thought of the line in the basement notebook — *a curio connects a person to memory, not a commodity symbol* — and what Aether did was exactly pull the symbols out of memory and replace them with a price tag.

"And the word *healing* is the worst part," Mei said. "We never claim a curio cures anything. Incense is scent-memory; a stone is a psychological anchor. But they write *therapeutic frequency*, *blessed* — that's a lie."

"Call it what it is," Zhou said — a rare cold laugh.

Elena listened, her face flushing and paling. "I… I just thought, maybe it would really work."
"You wanting it to work is not wrong," Mei took her hand. "Only this one isn't on the side that could help you."

## III. So We Open a Class

That evening, three of them gathered under the jade lantern. Noah stopped by too, two coffees in hand; hearing the story, he slapped a note on the table. "What you're missing isn't a discount. It's someone showing people what *real* means."

And so the "True Curios Class" was set — once a week, inside Jade Lantern Curio, no selling, only origins: where this stone comes from, why that incense is lit, what culture sits behind a symbol instead of what price tag hangs below it.

As she spoke, Mei took down a **Cinnabar Vitality Pendant** (p03) — brass under a coat of cinnabar-red lacquer, a small warm fire that wouldn't go out in the lamplight. She held it up to Elena. "Look at this red. In East Asia it isn't a random printed decoration — it's the color of protection and life force, of carrying a warm ember of confidence wherever you go. The real thing speaks of lineage and intention, not neon and *matrix*."

On the whiteboard, Mei wrote one line: *Lineage > Function.*

"We can't out-shout their neon," Zhou said.
"Then don't out-shout it. Out-last it." Mei stuck the first class poster to the window — plain white paper, ink characters, a small jade lantern drawn in the corner, facing Aether across the street.

Outside the door, the obsidian bead swayed gently in the wind, as if it nodded.
